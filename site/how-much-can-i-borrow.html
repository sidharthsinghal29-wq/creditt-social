How much can I borrow? What lenders actually look at

Your borrowing power isn't a fixed multiple of your salary. Each lender runs its own calculation, which is why the same person can get quite different answers from different banks. Here's what goes into it.

1. Your income

Base salary is counted in full. Overtime, bonuses, commissions and allowances are often counted only in part, or averaged over one or two years. Self-employed income is assessed from tax returns. See self-employed home loans. Rental income is usually counted at a reduced percentage.

2. Your living expenses

Lenders use the higher of your declared expenses and a benchmark for your household size and income. Bank statements are checked, so be realistic.

3. Existing debts and credit limits

Car loans, personal loans, HECS-HELP and buy now pay later all reduce borrowing power. Credit cards count at their limit, not their balance, so cancelling unused cards or lowering limits can help.

4. The serviceability buffer

Lenders test whether you could afford repayments at a rate a few percentage points above the actual rate, under guidance from the banking regulator APRA. Banks also limit how much of their lending goes to borrowers with very high debt compared with income.

5. Dependants and household

Children and other dependants increase the expense benchmark and reduce borrowing power.

Ways to improve your borrowing power

  • Pay down or close high-interest debts and reduce credit card limits.
  • Choose a lender whose income policy suits you, for example one that counts more of your bonus or rental income.
  • Consider a longer loan term or a co-borrower, understanding the trade-offs.
