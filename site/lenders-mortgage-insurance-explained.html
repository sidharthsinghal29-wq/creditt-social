Lenders mortgage insurance (LMI) explained

Lenders mortgage insurance (LMI) is a one-off insurance premium you usually pay when you borrow more than 80% of a property's value. It protects the lender, not you, if the loan isn't repaid and the sale of the property doesn't cover the debt. It can cost from around 1% to several percent of the loan amount, but it can also let you buy years sooner than saving a full 20% deposit. Here's how it works and how to reduce or avoid it.

What LMI is and who it protects

When your deposit is under 20%, the lender is taking on more risk. LMI is the insurance policy that covers that risk. If you default and the property sells for less than you owe, the insurer pays the lender's shortfall. ASIC's Moneysmart is clear that LMI gives the borrower no direct benefit, and lenders such as CommBank note that the insurer can still try to recover that shortfall from the borrower and any guarantor.

LMI is different from mortgage protection insurance, which is an optional policy that helps cover your repayments if you're sick, injured or lose your job.

When you'll pay LMI

The trigger is your loan-to-value ratio (LVR): the loan amount as a percentage of the property's value. Most lenders charge LMI once the LVR goes above 80%. For example, buying a $1,000,000 home with a $150,000 deposit means borrowing $850,000, an LVR of 85%, so LMI would normally apply.

Keep in mind the lender uses its own valuation, not just the price you paid. If the bank values the property below the contract price, your LVR goes up and LMI can apply even when you thought you had 20%.

How much LMI costs

There's no single price. The premium depends on:

  • Your LVR. The higher it is, the higher the premium, and the jump from 90% to 95% is usually large.
  • The loan size. Bigger loans attract bigger premiums, which matters in Sydney.
  • The loan type and borrower profile. Investment loans and some self-employed or low doc loans can cost more.
  • The lender and its insurer. Two lenders can quote quite different LMI for the same deal.

Major banks quote ranges of roughly 1% to 5% of the loan, and NAB notes it can reach up to 6.5%. In NSW, LMI on property is exempt from insurance duty for premiums paid since 1 July 2017, according to Revenue NSW, so there's no extra state duty on top.

Paying upfront or adding it to the loan

Most borrowers add (capitalise) the premium to the loan rather than paying cash at settlement. That keeps more of your savings for stamp duty and other costs, but you then pay interest on the LMI over the life of the loan. Capitalising also nudges your LVR higher, so check that the total still fits within the lender's maximum. Our repayment calculator can show the difference a larger loan makes.

Ways to avoid or reduce LMI

  • Government 5% Deposit Scheme. Eligible first home buyers can buy with a 5% deposit (or 2% for eligible single parents) with no LMI, because the government guarantees part of the loan. There are no income caps and no limit on places, and price caps apply. See our NSW first home buyer guide for details.
  • Family guarantee. A parent can use equity in their own home as extra security, which can bring your LVR to 80% or below and remove LMI. It does put their property at risk, so they should get independent advice.
  • Professional LMI waivers. Some lenders waive LMI for eligible professionals in medical, legal and financial fields. Eligibility, LVR limits and loan sizes vary between lenders.
  • Save a little more. Getting from 90% to 85% LVR can cut the premium noticeably, so run the numbers before you decide.
  • Compare lenders. Because premiums and valuations differ, the same purchase can carry different LMI costs depending on where you apply.

LMI when you refinance or sell

LMI is tied to the original loan. It can't be transferred to a new lender, so if you refinance while your LVR is still above 80%, you may have to pay LMI again. Some lenders offer a partial refund if you close the loan early: NAB, for instance, refunds part of the premium on loans closed within 12 months under its current terms. After that, there's usually nothing back.

If you're thinking about refinancing, check your current LVR first. Paying down the loan or a rise in your property's value can bring you under 80%, which often makes switching much easier. For investors using equity, the same rule applies to top-ups; see investment loans.

Is paying LMI worth it?

Sometimes, yes. If prices or rents rise while you save, waiting for 20% can cost more than the premium itself. Other times a family guarantee, a government scheme or a waiver will save you the cost outright. The right answer depends on your deposit, income, the property and the lenders available to you.
