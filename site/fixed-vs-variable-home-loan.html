Fixed vs variable home loan: how to decide

There's no universally better choice between a fixed and variable home loan. A fixed rate gives you certain repayments for a set term, usually one to five years, but limits extra repayments and can cost a lot to exit early. A variable rate moves up and down, but usually comes with offset, redraw and the freedom to repay or refinance at any time. The right choice depends on how much certainty your budget needs, and how likely you are to sell, refinance or pay down the loan during the fixed period. Many borrowers split the difference.

How fixed and variable rates work

A variable rate can change at any time. It tends to follow the Reserve Bank's cash rate, but ASIC's Moneysmart points out lenders can also move it independently. Variable rates usually follow when the RBA moves, as it did in November 2026 when it lifted the cash rate to 4.85%.

A fixed rate is locked for an agreed term, so your repayment stays the same even if rates rise. The flip side is that if rates fall, you keep paying the higher fixed rate. Lenders set fixed rates based on where they expect funding costs to go, so a fixed rate can be higher or lower than the variable rate on offer at any given time.

What you give up when you fix

  • Extra repayments are capped. Many fixed loans limit extra repayments before fees apply. One major bank, for example, allows up to $10,000 a year on its fixed loans.
  • Offset and redraw may be limited or unavailable. If you hold savings, this can matter more than the rate itself. Our guide to offset vs redraw explains the difference.
  • Break costs. If you repay, sell or refinance during the fixed term, the lender can charge a break cost to cover its loss. Moneysmart warns these can be large, especially if rates have fallen since you fixed.
  • The roll-off rate. At the end of the term, the loan usually reverts to the lender's standard variable rate, which may not be its most competitive.

Break costs and why they matter in Sydney

Break costs are calculated on the amount and time left in the fixed term, and on how far wholesale rates have moved since you fixed. Bigger loans mean bigger potential break costs, and Sydney loan sizes are among the largest in the country. Because the cost depends on market movements, your lender can only give you an estimate on the day you ask.

A common issue we see as brokers is people fixing for five years, then upgrading, separating, or moving for work two years in. Selling a home in NSW already involves agent fees, and buying the next one brings NSW stamp duty, so an unexpected break cost on top can hurt. Before you fix, be honest about how likely a sale or refinance is within the term.

Split loans: some of both

A split loan divides your borrowing into a fixed portion and a variable portion. You get repayment certainty on part of the debt, while the variable part keeps offset, redraw and unlimited extra repayments. Moneysmart gives the example of fixing $200,000 of a $300,000 loan and keeping $100,000 variable.

There's no right ratio. A practical approach is to fix the amount you're confident you won't pay down early, and keep anything you might reduce with bonuses, savings or a future sale on variable. You can refine the split with our repayment calculator.

Rate locks: protecting the fixed rate you were quoted

Fixed rates can change between your application and settlement. Unless you pay for a rate lock, most lenders apply the fixed rate on the day the loan settles, not the day you applied. A rate lock holds the rate for a set period for a fee; that same bank, for example, charges $750 to lock for 90 days. It's worth weighing up if settlement is weeks away and fixed rates are moving, which is common with Sydney purchases on longer settlements.

Questions to help you decide

  • Could your budget handle a rate rise of 1% or 2%? If that would be a real strain, fixing some or all of the loan gives breathing room.
  • Do you expect to sell, upgrade or refinance in the next few years? If so, variable or a shorter fixed term limits break cost risk.
  • Will you receive bonuses, an inheritance or a business payout you'd like to put into the loan? Variable makes that easy.
  • Do you hold significant savings? An offset on a variable loan may save more than a fixed rate discount.
  • Is this an investment loan? Investors often weigh cash flow certainty differently from owner-occupiers.

Whatever you choose, lenders still check you could afford repayments at a rate at least 3 percentage points above your loan rate, the serviceability buffer APRA has kept in place through 2026. Fixing doesn't increase how much you can borrow; see how much can I borrow for what lenders assess.

If your fixed rate is about to expire

Start reviewing two to three months before the end date. Ask your lender what rate you'll roll onto, then compare that with refixing or a competitive variable loan elsewhere. Switching at expiry avoids break costs entirely, which makes it one of the best times to refinance. Our refinancing checklist covers the documents you'll need. Common questions

No one can reliably predict where rates will go, so the better question is what you need from the loan. Fixing suits borrowers who value certain repayments and won't need to sell, refinance or pay the loan down quickly. Variable suits those who want offset, unlimited extra repayments and the freedom to move.

The loan usually rolls onto the lender's variable rate for that loan type, which may not be its sharpest rate. Review your options a couple of months before expiry so you can refix, switch to a better variable product or refinance without a gap.

Some lenders offer a partial or full offset on fixed loans, but many don't. A common workaround is a split loan, with the offset attached to the variable portion.
