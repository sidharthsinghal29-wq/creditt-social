Guarantor home loans: how a family guarantee works

A guarantor home loan (often called a family guarantee) lets a close family member, usually a parent, offer equity in their own property as extra security for your loan. That extra security can let you buy with a small deposit and avoid lenders mortgage insurance (LMI), but the guarantor takes on real risk: if you can't repay, the lender can ask them to pay up to the guaranteed amount, and in the worst case their own home could be at stake. Here's how it works, who can be a guarantor, and what both sides should check before signing.

How a family guarantee works

Most lenders want a 20% deposit before they'll lend without LMI. With a family guarantee, the lender takes a second mortgage (or a guarantee secured over a term deposit) on the guarantor's property to cover the gap between your deposit and that 20% mark. The guarantor doesn't hand over cash and doesn't make your repayments. You still borrow the full loan and you're still the one who has to repay it.

As an illustration only: you're buying a $1,000,000 home in Sydney with a $50,000 (5%) deposit. Your parents guarantee $150,000 (15%), secured against equity in their home. Combined, the lender sees the equivalent of a 20% deposit, so LMI may not apply. You'd still need funds for stamp duty, legal costs and any lender fees, because the guarantee covers security, not cash.

Limited vs full guarantees

A limited guarantee caps what the guarantor is liable for, typically the portion of the loan above 80% of the property's value. This is the most common structure, and Moneysmart suggests guaranteeing a fixed amount rather than the whole loan where possible.

A full or unlimited guarantee makes the guarantor responsible for the entire loan plus interest and costs. It's less common for home buyers and carries much more risk, so it's worth asking exactly which type a lender is offering.

Some lenders also have related arrangements, such as a guarantee secured by a family member's term deposit, or spousal guarantees. Names and rules differ between lenders, so compare the actual documents rather than the product name.

Who can be a guarantor?

Guarantors are usually close family: parents most often, and with some lenders grandparents, step-parents or adult siblings. Rules vary between lenders, but a guarantor generally needs to:

  • own property with enough usable equity to cover the guarantee (an existing mortgage on it doesn't always rule them out)
  • have a clean credit history and be able to show they can meet their own commitments
  • understand the legal obligations and risks
  • get independent legal advice before signing, which most lenders require

Lenders may also look at the guarantor's age and stage of life, for example whether they're retired or relying on a pension, because that affects their ability to cover the guarantee if it's ever called on.

The risks for the guarantor

ASIC's Moneysmart is blunt about this: a guarantor can be asked to pay if the borrower can't, and if their home is the security the lender could ultimately sell it. Other effects are easier to overlook:

  • Their own borrowing. Lenders ask whether you're a guarantor on other loans, and the guarantee can reduce how much the guarantor can borrow for a renovation, investment or downsizing move while it's in place.
  • Their credit file. If a guaranteed loan goes into default, the guarantor's credit report can be affected too.
  • Getting out early. Once signed, a guarantor generally can't simply withdraw. Release depends on the lender agreeing.

Moneysmart's advice for guarantors includes getting a copy of the loan contract beforehand, asking plenty of questions, and working out whether they could actually meet the repayments. Feeling pressured to sign is a warning sign. If someone feels pushed into it, a free financial counsellor (National Debt Helpline, 1800 007 007) is a good first call.

Getting the guarantee released

A family guarantee is usually meant to be temporary. Once your loan falls to around 80% of the property's value, through repayments, extra payments or a rise in the property's value, you can ask the lender to release the guarantor. That normally means a new valuation and a fresh credit assessment of you alone, so your income and repayment history matter. If your current lender won't release it, refinancing to another lender on your own can achieve the same result.

A common issue we see as brokers is families agreeing to "a few years" without a plan. It helps to agree on a target, for example aiming to release the guarantee once the loan reaches 80% of value, and to check progress each year.

Family guarantee vs the 5% Deposit Scheme in Sydney

For first home buyers, the Australian Government 5% Deposit Scheme also allows a 5% deposit with no LMI, without involving anyone else's property. In Sydney the price cap is $1.5 million, there are no income caps, and the home must be your own residence. Our NSW first home buyer guide covers the scheme and NSW stamp duty concessions (no duty up to $800,000 for eligible buyers, reduced duty under $1 million).

A family guarantee can make more sense if you're above the scheme's price cap, aren't a first home buyer, want to borrow with less than 5% saved, or want to cover more of the upfront costs. It isn't limited to first homes, although some lenders restrict it to homes you will live in. The trade-off is the risk to your family, which the government scheme avoids.

Practical tips before you apply

  • Work out your own borrowing power first. A guarantee helps with the deposit, not with whether you can afford the repayments. See how much can I borrow or try our borrowing power calculator.
  • Ask each lender for the exact guarantee amount, the type of guarantee and how release works.
  • Have the guarantor get their own independent legal advice, separate from yours.
  • Talk openly as a family about what happens if your circumstances change, such as job loss, separation or illness.
